Tempo

The timing coaches actually watch for — how long you spend at each phase of the delivery. This is just counting frames between visually-identified checkpoints, so it holds up fine even from an imperfect camera angle, unlike a computed joint angle would.

Auto-detected foot strike & release look off? Adjust here — it updates the timing below.

Kinetic chain

This is the actual energy-transfer question: do your hips, shoulders, and arm speed up in the right order, each building on the one before it, or is the chain compressed and firing all at once? This is about timing and sequence, not absolute angles, so it's much less sensitive to camera angle than a joint-angle measurement would be.

Side by side

Synced so foot strike lines up in both clips. Drag the shared scrubber to step through the delivery together — that's a frame-accurate comparison. "Play both" is best-effort and can drift a little, since these are two independent video files.

In overlay mode, the compare clip's skeleton (cyan) is scaled and centered to your delivery's hips and torso length each frame — it lines up position and size, not camera angle or lens differences, so treat it as a visual guide, not a precise measurement.

Checklist

Run your own eye over it — this is exactly what a coach looks for on the field, no lab required. Nothing here is computed; it's here to give the self-review some structure.

AI summary

This doesn't measure anything new — it takes the tempo, the kinetic chain readout, and whatever you've filled in on the checklist above, and asks Claude to write it up as an actual narrative instead of numbers sitting side by side. Only as good as what's fed into it, and it can't see anything the data above doesn't already show.

Drills by focus area

Not auto-triggered by a score — pick whichever area your own eye (or the checklist above) flagged. Each is sourced to a real training program, not a generic list.

A note on plyo and weighted-ball work: a few of the drills below use Plyocare-style or weighted balls. These are standard at the college and pro level, but published research has also linked weighted-ball overload/underload throwing to higher rates of arm and trunk injury when progression isn't managed carefully. Most programs restrict that kind of loaded throwing to pitchers who are roughly 16 and up and skeletally mature, built up gradually, and ideally overseen by a coach or physical therapist.
This version deliberately doesn't measure or score joint angles. A single 2D phone camera can't reliably do that — the same real angle looks different depending on how square the camera is to the pitcher, and no amount of smoothing or model upgrades fully fixes that. What it does instead: times the delivery by detecting the same visual checkpoints a coach would (leg lift, foot strike, release), and helps you compare two clips side by side or overlaid. Foot strike and release are still detected automatically and can still be wrong on a given clip — use the adjustment controls above if the markers look off. The overlay view aligns position and size, not camera angle, so differences you see there can come from filming setup as much as from mechanics. Drill suggestions are a starting point, not a personalized program — check with a coach or physical therapist before changing your training, especially before adding weighted-ball or plyo work.
